Soft Corner Protection & Weather Seals

Status: Unreviewed. This is a preliminary design concept. The proposed material combinations and sealing details have not yet been reviewed or tested on the actual HUB75 enclosure.

A corner protector can have two different tasks:

  1. Protect the display and any surface it touches (a table, floor or another case) from knocks and scratches.
  2. Help keep rainwater away from seams and the electronics.

These tasks are related, but a soft corner alone cannot seal the entire enclosure. This page explores how to combine PCTG (or another rigid printable material), TPU and a conventional rubber gasket without confusing their functions.

Project context: the HUB75 display has five panels and a wooden enclosure concept. It goes outdoors about once per year, not permanently. A rear-assembly enclosure and a removable-top enclosure are both still under consideration. The objective is practical rain resistance, not a claimed waterproof/IP rating.

The material roles

PartCandidatePurpose
Internal brackets and panel fittingsPETGHolds and locates the display’s internal structure
External corner or connectorPCTG; PETG or ASA are alternativesTakes handling loads and protects the enclosure’s edges
Soft contact pad / bumperTPU 95A, first material to tryKeeps the harder corner away from a table or other object; adds some cushioning
Seam gasketEPDM or silicone strip/cordForms a continuous compressed water barrier at the opening

No evidence is available here that ASA is less likely than PCTG to scratch another surface. Both are hard plastics relative to TPU. A softer contact pad is a more direct response to that risk. Also, trapped dust or sand can still scratch through a soft pad; contact surfaces should stay clean.

TPU 95A is a good first printable bumper material, but its flexibility depends strongly on thickness and geometry. A thick, solid TPU print can feel surprisingly firm. See the Prusa TPU 95A guide.

Rigid material + TPU: three combinations

The flexible pad can be fitted to different rigid materials. The pad, rather than the choice between these rigid plastics, addresses the risk of scratching other objects.

CombinationMain reason to choose itOpen point
PETG + TPUStraightforward, easy-to-print and economical first prototypeIs PETG strong and heat-stable enough at the exposed corner?
PCTG + TPUCombines an impact-tough external corner with a soft contact faceIs the extra toughness worth the material cost and printing effort?
ASA + TPUUseful when the rigid part needs better heat / sunlight toleranceMore difficult to print; no demonstrated advantage for avoiding scratches

All three still need a suitable separate gasket at an opening. TPU is not necessarily bonded to the rigid plastic: a mechanically retained insert lets each component be printed and replaced separately.

How the two functions fit together

Project-owned concept sketches; not to scale. They illustrate two separate locations on the same display, not a finished enclosure cross-section.

  • Left: the PCTG corner is the rigid mounting/protective part. A small, replaceable TPU contact piece is the first material to touch a table or another object.
  • Right: an EPDM or silicone profile lies between the enclosure body and the closing panel. The lid presses it evenly around the whole opening. The soft external bumper is not the primary water seal.

Why not use the TPU bumper itself as the gasket?

A corner pad does not cover the entire seam. A single printed TPU component combining a bumper and a long sealing lip is possible to experiment with, but it raises additional issues: layer lines, seams between print sections, retention, uneven compression and replacing a damaged section.

Prusa’s water-tight 3D-printing tests found their printed flexible gaskets unreliable, even under heavy compression. Conventional O-rings worked far better in their test assemblies. A separate study does describe ways to make TPU seals work with carefully smoothed mating faces, so a printed TPU gasket is not impossible, but should be treated as an experimental part that requires leak testing, not as a guaranteed waterproof seal: practical laboratory printing guide.

Three design approaches

A. Separate PCTG corner + TPU bumper + rubber seal — preferred

How it works

  1. Print the structural or protective corner in PCTG. Internal attachments can remain PETG.
  2. Give the outside of that corner a shallow recess, keyway or retaining lip for a small TPU contact pad.
  3. Print the TPU pad as a separate part. Ideally retain it mechanically so that it cannot slide off or peel away during handling. Adhesive can be considered, but should not be the only untested retention method.
  4. Use a continuous EPDM or silicone gasket in the actual lid/back-panel seam. The gasket must be compressed when the opening is closed.
  5. Make the seal continuous at corners. Do not let screws, corner fixings or gaps bypass it.

Why this is the starting choice: each material has one clear job. The soft pad can be replaced without changing the corner, and the rubber seal can be specified and tested independently. There is no requirement for a multi-material print.

B. PCTG corner + TPU wraparound lip — interesting alternative

A TPU piece could extend around the external corner or cover a little more of the edge. Its flexible overhang can act as a rain deflector and reduce the amount of water reaching a joint.

However, a rain deflector is not a compressed seal. Water can travel behind the TPU, through fastening holes or along another seam. A continuous EPDM/silicone gasket remains the more dependable primary seal around an access opening.

This version is worth exploring if the external appearance benefits from a continuous soft edge, not merely pads at the corners.

C. Integrated printed TPU bumper and gasket — experimental

One printed TPU piece could combine the contact protection with a sealing lip, retained by a PCTG corner/frame.

It could reduce the number of separate pieces, but only if a continuous sealing path and controlled compression can be maintained across corners, joins and fasteners. A TPU 95A lip may be too stiff for an uneven wooden opening. It is more difficult to print, fit, inspect and replace than approach A.

Keep as a later experiment, not the baseline.

Practical sealing details

  • Continuous path: follow the entire opening; a strip stopping just short of a corner creates a leak path. Joined ends and mitred corners need a suitable joint or adhesive method.
  • Compression: the lid or rear panel must push uniformly on the gasket. Follow the chosen seal’s datasheet for compression, and consider a positive stop to avoid overtightening and squashing it flat.
  • Flat sealing land: painted or sealed plywood edges must be even and stable. Rough, uneven surfaces make sealing harder; the gasket cannot compensate for unlimited gaps.
  • Wood: finish exposed plywood edges and holes separately; the soft parts do not protect wet, unsealed plywood.
  • Cable entries: use cable glands or another tested entry arrangement; do not assume a TPU pad seals cables.
  • Front window: check the acrylic-to-frame perimeter independently; the PCTG corner does not seal it.
  • Water versus cooling: a rain-resistant enclosure may need a deliberate plan for heat and condensation; do not seal ventilation without checking panel and power-supply temperatures.
  • No IP claim yet: an IP rating follows testing of the finished enclosure and cannot be inferred just from a chosen gasket material.

For examples of real enclosure gasket attachment and material choices, see 3M: sealing and gasketing and 3M: gasket attachment.

First prototypes

Rather than printing a whole five-panel enclosure, first make two small independent test parts.

Prototype 1 — scratch and impact protection

  • Make a representative PCTG corner and TPU 95A insert.
  • Try an insert with a mechanical retention feature (recess/tab, dovetail or captured edge) so it can be replaced.
  • Ensure TPU—not exposed PCTG, a print seam or a screw head—is the part that touches a table.
  • Put a sample on a clean, representative surface and check for scratches, scuff marks, TPU residue and pad movement during repeated placement/sliding. Repeat only after inspecting and cleaning for abrasive grit.
  • Check whether the TPU can cushion a moderate knock while the PCTG corner still stays securely fastened. This is a functional test, not a calibrated drop-test certification.

Start with the chosen printer’s TPU-compatible profile; do not assume that a hard TPU 95A insert will be soft enough merely because it is called TPU.

Prototype 2 — rain seal

  • Make a short section of the actual planned lid or rear-panel joint, including the chosen plywood finish and fastening method.
  • Try a standard EPDM/silicone gasket with suitable backing/retention and a defined compression gap.
  • Verify contact and compression along the entire section, especially around a corner.
  • Spray water from the expected rain directions, with absorbent paper inside the test section; inspect for leaks and trapped water. Repeat after opening and re-closing it.
  • Only then test the complete, unpowered enclosure, before any electronics are installed. Do not spray water on an energised or unprotected display.

Decisions still open

  1. Rear access or removable top? This determines where the main continuous seal goes.
  2. TPU only at four corners, or along more of the outside edge? Start with separate corner pads unless more contact surfaces need protection.
  3. How to retain the TPU pad? Compare a captured insert with a removable snap-fit; the final shape depends on the actual PCTG corner.
  4. Which EPDM/silicone profile? Pick a real product and its compression specification before assigning groove dimensions in CAD.

Current conclusion

Start with A: PETG inside, PCTG outside, replaceable TPU contact protection, and a separate continuous EPDM/silicone seal on access openings. ASA remains a comparison option, not a solution to scratch marks by itself.

The display is outdoors only once a year. Prioritise protection during transport/handling, preventing damage to surfaces, and a carefully tested rain-resistant enclosure. Avoid claiming the display is waterproof before the housing, window and cable entries have all been tested.
